> **Editor's note:** A detailed guide to common issues programmers face when publishing a book. Recommended for anyone considering writing one.
>
> **Original article:** <https://www.cnblogs.com/JavaArchitect/p/14128202.html>

The ancient Chinese tradition speaks of three ways to leave a lasting legacy: virtue, achievement, and writing. A programmer publishing a book may not sound quite so lofty, but it is still a worthwhile endeavor.

Publishing a book is not very profitable, and the time from starting to write until receiving payment can be long. Still, having your own technical book can demonstrate your expertise in interviews and gradually build your reputation in the industry. It can also give you confidence in interviews and other professional situations. Here, I draw on my experience and mistakes to discuss what programmers should know about publishing a book.

## 1. Royalties and the Time Required

First, let us consider the actual payment and effort involved, setting aside the less tangible benefits of publishing.

If you work directly with a publisher, payment is usually a royalty: a percentage of the book's list price multiplied by the print run or the number of copies sold. The rate is often 8%; well-known authors may negotiate more, but a typical upper range is 10% to 12%. Note that the calculation uses the full list price, not the discounted price.

For example, suppose a book has a list price of CNY 70 and sells at a 30% discount on JD.com or another store. At an 8% royalty, the author receives CNY 5.60 per copy, before tax.

Check whether the contract calculates royalties from the print run or actual sales. My publisher contracts usually use the print run. A typical first print run for a computer book is 2,500 copies, which at CNY 70 and 8% yields `70 × 8% × 2,500` before tax. If royalties are based on sales and only 1,800 copies sell, the payment is calculated on 1,800 copies instead.

A 300-page book may be priced around CNY 70. At an 8% royalty and a 2,500-copy print run, the gross income is CNY 14,000, or about CNY 12,000 after tax. A first-time author may spend at least eight months writing it. That averages about CNY 1,500 per month—not much.

I cannot speak for other authors, but what else did I gain from publishing besides royalties?

- In interviews at my current and former companies, mentioning a related book I had published made interviewers consider me experienced and saved me time explaining my background.
- I teach in-person courses and use my recently published Python book as the course material, so I do not have to prepare everything from scratch.
- When discussing a project, my book can demonstrate my technical ability. This is especially effective when meeting someone for the first time.

The first benefit can be particularly useful when applying to a small company or a contractor role. A candidate with a book in the relevant area may even be hired without an interview. I received this treatment when interviewing for a contractor role at a large company.

## 2. When Royalties Are Paid and What Reprints Earn

I worked directly with publishers. Usually, within three months of the first printing, I received part of the royalty for that print run—typically 50% to 90%. I received the remainder about a year after publication. Many books are fortunate to sell through their first print run, though some are reprinted or reach a second or third edition. Royalties for reprints are generally settled six months to a year after the reprint.

Payment is delayed, and the royalties are modest compared with the author's labor. Publishing is not really a way to make money, and payment takes a long time. If an agency provides little help during publishing but also takes a cut, that can feel unfair to the author's hard work.

## 3. What I Learned from Book Agencies

Before working with publisher editors, I also spoke with staff at book agencies. Many treated me respectfully. The conversations were polite, though not especially in-depth. In the end, I chose not to publish through an agency because the terms offered by publishers were better. Here are some specific experiences.

- Staff from book agencies often left messages on sites such as Cnblogs asking whether I wanted to publish a book. Unless I asked, they generally did not say whether they were publisher editors or agency staff. Some agency staff tell authors, especially first-time authors, that “publisher editors generally do not contact authors directly” and that “books are usually published through an agency.” These claims are not entirely false: if you do not contact an editor, the editor will not contact you. But contacting an editor directly is neither difficult nor necessarily less effective.
- When I discussed outlines with publisher editors, they gave specific revision advice even when the outline needed work—for example, what a chapter should cover or how to structure a section. Some agency staff I spoke to mostly said, “Rewrite it,” without explaining how. Their feedback was abstract, leaving me to figure everything out. I described a specific experience in my earlier article, [How Can a Programmer Publish a Technical Book?](./how-do-programmers-publish-a-technical-book.md).
- I have never signed a publishing agreement with a book agency because our conversations did not go very far, but I know that only a publisher can publish a book. I cannot say from experience whether agency contracts contain clauses that manage risks. However, I saw an agency representative share cases where manuscripts were rejected and seem to blame the authors. That did not sit right with me. The representative did not catch and report problems promptly, failed to coordinate a solution, and then criticized the author after rejection—even though the author had done the work and the agency bore none of the risk. Authors may share some responsibility for a rejection, but as an author myself, I felt sympathy for them. When I worked with publishers, editors sometimes checked in proactively and provided materials. If a problem came up, they helped address it quickly, so major rewrites were rare.
- Agencies' royalty terms also vary. I have seen per-page payments of CNY 30–50 with a full buyout, which means authors receive no more royalties if the book is reprinted. I have also seen a 6% royalty. I have not seen whether an agency might offer 8% or more, so I cannot say.

I have not spoken with many agency staff or had in-depth discussions with them; I now mainly work with publisher editors. These are my impressions of a few agencies, not a judgment of all of them. Some of the agency staff I spoke with were at least very respectful. You can compare working with an agency and working directly with a publisher. In any case, before writing a book or signing an agreement, clarify the following. The other party should make these facts clear to you:

- Ask whether the person is a publisher editor or an agency representative. They should disclose this proactively.
- Find out which publisher will publish the book. The agreement should name the publisher; it should not be decided after you finish the manuscript. Only a publisher—not an agency—can publish the book.
- Clarify how royalties are calculated. Even if an agency takes a cut, you should know the royalty offered by the publisher. If you publish through an agency, the publisher still pays the agency the agreed amount; the difference is likely the agency's revenue.
- Confirm who will sign the publishing contract: the agency or the publisher. Understand this before signing. Even if you sign with an agency, find out whether you could sign directly with the publisher.
- Do not assume that agencies have lower standards or that you can only publish through one because you are not good enough. An agency cannot publish the book itself; it still has to submit the manuscript to a publisher. The standards will not be lower. If a publisher editor rejects your outline, an agency representative will likely do the same, even if you ask for a lower royalty.

If you understand the difference between an agency and a publisher and still choose the agency, that is a decision between both parties. If the agency does not disclose the difference and you work with it without understanding the distinction, you cannot be expected to know better. It helps to hear both sides: if you plan to publish, talk to agencies and publishers and compare the options.

## 4. How to Contact Editors at Established Chinese Computer-Book Publishers

I have published with Tsinghua University Press, China Machine Press, Peking University Press, and Publishing House of Electronics Industry. The process was smooth, and my interactions with editors were pleasant. I do not want to rank domestic publishers, but the four best-known publishers in the computer field are Tsinghua, China Machine Press, Publishing House of Electronics Industry, and Posts & Telecom Press. Other publishers have also released excellent computer books.

How can you contact editors at these publishers?

- Visit the publisher's official website; contact information is usually listed there.
- Publish articles on sites such as Cnblogs. People may contact you about a book, including both agency staff and publisher editors. Publisher editors generally introduce themselves directly, for example, “I am an editor at XX Press.”
- I have also contacted some publisher editors myself. I can share their contact details if needed.

How do you find agency staff? Usually, you do not need to look for them. After you publish several blog posts, they will contact you. If you ask whether someone is a publisher editor or an agency representative, they will tell you. If you ask further, they may explain the difference between publishers and agencies from the agency's perspective.

As you can see, finding editors at established publishers is not difficult, whether or not you ultimately write a book. After you make contact, they can also discuss potential topics with you.

## 5. Choosing a Topic and the Publishing Process

Here is the process I followed when working with publisher editors to publish a book.

First, after contacting an editor, discuss the topic. Choose an area you know well or want to specialize in, such as Java distributed components, the Spring Cloud ecosystem, microservices, Python data analysis, machine learning, or deep learning. Strong project experience is helpful. If you are not familiar with the topic yet but are willing to study systematically and produce a coherent book that helps readers, you can still write about it.

Second, once you have chosen a topic, draft an outline. For a book on Python data analysis, for example, you might plan 12 chapters, starting with Python syntax and then covering topics such as NumPy. You can consult the tables of contents of other books while planning. When the editor approves your outline, you can agree on the publishing terms.

Publishing agreements are similar for most authors. Royalties are often around 8%; the writing schedule is negotiated with the publisher, and payment schedules are usually similar. Publishers generally buy the electronic and other-language rights. Well-known authors may be able to negotiate these terms.

Then comes the writing, which can be tedious, especially in the final chapters. I usually write for half an hour each weekday and four or five hours over the weekend. That is enough to finish a 300-page book in about six months. I discuss ways to write efficiently later in this article.

While writing, it is generally a good idea to send each completed chapter to the editor for review. This helps prevent larger problems. First-time authors also need time to develop their wording and writing skills; publisher editors can help from the beginning.

After submitting the complete manuscript, it may go through three rounds of proofreading and review. The editors I work with help fix grammar and typos, then send me a list of revisions to confirm and make. From what I have learned, the risk of rejection for books published through an agency often comes at this stage, because an agency may submit the entire manuscript to the publisher all at once. I submit each chapter directly to the publisher's editor, so major problems are usually identified and fixed in the first few chapters. The final revision list is therefore shorter. In other words, if you work directly with a publisher, the three rounds of proofreading and review may not require too much work. After submitting a book, I usually let the editor handle that process while I plan and begin writing my next one.

Finally, there are royalties. As mentioned earlier, authors should not expect much. But if you happen to write a bestseller that sells 5,000 or even 10,000 copies, you might earn around CNY 50,000 in additional income within a year and build some recognition in the industry.

## 6. Case-Study Books Are Faster to Write Than Experience-Based Books

Publishing is difficult for some authors, especially beginners. They may start enthusiastically, then encounter accumulating problems, get busy at work, and abandon the project—or take more than a year to finish. In my view, a 300- to 400-page book should take no more than eight months to write. To meet that schedule, I suggest that first-time authors start with a case-study book rather than a book about their accumulated experience.

What is a case-study book? One approach is to use a single large project throughout the book to teach a topic, such as mini-program or full-stack development. Another is to include one case study in each chapter, such as ten Python deep-learning examples. An experience-based book, by contrast, might cover interview advice or the practical experience of an expert in distributed, high-concurrency development.

I am not saying that one type of book is better than the other. Case-study books are simply easier for first-time authors. They rely more on explaining concrete examples: introduce a case, such as image recognition in Python deep learning; explain the APIs it uses, such as those from a Python library; and cover the relevant technical details, such as how to build an image-recognition feature from several library components. Case-study books also require less personal interpretation. Authors do not have to organize their experience into their own words. Beginners may understand an experience but struggle to explain it clearly, which can weaken the book and delay writing.

Case studies are often easier to draw from existing examples. Explaining established technology is generally easier than explaining your own experience, and similar books can serve as references. Beginners therefore spend less time searching for the right wording and may finish a book in six to eight months. After writing a few books and gaining experience, they can try an experience-based book, which may then have a better chance of selling well.

Here is a practical structure for a case-study book:

- Start with a few chapters that explain environment setup and common fundamentals.
- Use a beginning–middle–end structure for each case-study chapter: introduce the requirements and technologies, explain how each feature is implemented, then summarize how to use the features.
- Use the same structure when explaining code: outline its overall structure, explain the key parts, then show the result and summarize the technical points.

At first, you might complete one chapter per month. As you become more practiced, you may be able to write two chapters a month. Finishing a book in eight months is achievable.

## 7. Avoiding Copyright Problems When Using Existing Material

Writing a book often involves consulting existing code and books, but that does not have to mean repeating their work. For example, an author could bring together case studies from several websites and teach Python data analysis systematically. The material may already exist, but readers benefit from having it organized in one place. Similarly, you might combine facial-recognition examples from several Python neural-network books and add your own features. That can be valuable to readers.

This raises copyright concerns. Authors must take them seriously. If a book has not yet been published, a copyright issue is bad enough; if it has already been published, the author may have to pay damages and suffer a lasting loss of reputation. Avoiding these problems is not difficult:

- Do not copy existing online content, even a single sentence. Rewrite it in your own words after understanding its meaning. Do not copy another book's table of contents or its text, either. Again, understand the source and express the ideas yourself.
- Do not copy other people's code from GitHub or anywhere else, even if it is open source. First understand and run the code. Then create your own project and implement your feature using what you learned, without copying and pasting large blocks. Your comments, variable names, class names, and method names should not simply duplicate the source. You can also add your own features.
- When explaining technical ideas and case studies, use your own words.

These practices let authors build on existing material, add their own features and insights, and publish a book of their own.

## 8. What First-Time Authors Should Keep in Mind

The previous sections covered the publishing process and how to write a case-study book. Here are some additional practical tips for first-time authors:

- Technical books differ from literary books. First, explain the technical concepts clearly. You can add engaging language afterward. Beginners can use straightforward prose to explain a case study instead of worrying too much about style.
- Write for beginners. Start with the basics rather than going too deep too soon. For a Python machine-learning book, begin with what machine learning is and how Python can be used for it. Starting with advanced practical experience may leave beginners behind.
- Beginners often want to write down everything they know. That enthusiasm is good, but consider what readers can realistically absorb. Before writing, set a clear goal—for example, a reader with no Python experience should be able to do useful work after reading the book. Keep the goal realistic; do not promise that a beginner will have three years of development experience afterward. Use the goal to shape the book and give fundamentals enough attention so readers can benefit.

That said, a first-time author who contacts a publisher's editor directly, picks a popular topic, and explains it through well-developed case studies may even write a bestseller that sells more than 10,000 copies.

## 9. Summary: Publishing with a Well-Known Chinese Publisher Takes Consistent Work

These days, running a social media account or making videos may earn more than publishing a book. But building an audience and producing videos also take time; they may not generate income quickly, or at all, if you do not publish content consistently. Publishing a book can be a useful first step: it helps you build a body of material and organize your knowledge. After that, monetizing the work through articles or videos may be much easier.

As described above, contacting publisher editors and choosing a topic are not difficult. If you write a case-study book and build on existing material, completing a regular book is not out of reach. Publishing is largely a matter of sustained effort. Finishing a book is possible if you stick with it; the question is whether you are willing to continue.

Once you have your own technical book, you can confidently tell interviewers that you are an expert in the subject. In your videos, articles, and other writing, you can introduce yourself as a computer-book author. More importantly, like a degree from a well-known university or experience at a major company, your book can provide evidence of your ability. By organizing your knowledge into a coherent body of work, you can confidently tell others—whether you are applying for a job, freelancing, or taking on a project—“I can do this.”
