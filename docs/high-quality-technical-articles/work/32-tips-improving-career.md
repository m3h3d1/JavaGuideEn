## The Fast Track to Growth

- Be humble when you first join, but don't use “I'm new” as a safety net.
- A technical blog post may take about two weeks to write, but it can be one of the fastest ways to grow.
- Read _The Pyramid Principle_ and _The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People_. The latter sounds like a self-help book, but it is really about building character.
- Keep asking what and why. Trace problems to their source and solve them; issues you try to avoid will be waiting around the next corner.
- Don't mistake busyness for security. Setting goals and pursuing them is a more reliable source of confidence.
- Don't obsess over short-term wins and losses. In a fair environment, accepting a temporary disadvantage can pay off; this isn't just a motivational slogan.
- Don't limit your thinking and skills to roles such as frontend, backend, or testing. Think of yourself as someone who solves problems in a business domain.
- Curiosity and passion are powerful shortcuts to growth. People who think long term see value in their work, even when the organization has not recognized it yet through KPIs.

## Put in the Work Every Day

- Make every line of code your best work at the time. A detail you consider unimportant could later count against you in a promotion review.
- A biweekly report isn't a daily work log. Don't just let time push you along; at minimum, know what you plan to accomplish before the next report. Let small goals guide you.
- If your daily work feels like a pile of trivial, nontechnical tasks or assisting a senior colleague, try grouping the work into categories. Picture each category as a box on the path to your goal. Then each day becomes a planned step toward that goal instead of mechanically completing requests. You may even volunteer for more work once you see where you are heading.
- Everyday behavior is a magnifying glass for your abilities. Many people don't realize how clearly their strengths and weaknesses show. Don't try to hide them; you can't fake your way through.

> The last point means that we sometimes focus too much on high-visibility moments, such as performance reviews, promotion panels, weekly reports, or presentations, thinking that people judge us by those moments. In reality, people judge how we handle daily work, help our colleagues, and create value. Anyone who has worked with us a few times can usually see our real qualities. A performance is only fooling ourselves.

## Learn to Work with Your Manager

> “Manager” and “supervisor” here refer broadly to people above you, including a product or project counterpart.

- Don't spread negative emotions or complain all the time.
- Be respectful but confident with your manager. Avoid challenging their views in public; discuss disagreements in private.
- Manage expectations upward, especially by aligning them early. If performance reviews come as a surprise, both sides are responsible, but you are the one who pays the price.
- Try to see things from your manager's perspective:
  - This can make decisions that once seemed baffling easier to understand.
  - Don't obsess over who does the work or gets the credit. Helping the team and easing your manager's burden matters far more for building trust.
  - Don't mistake this for blindly obeying your manager; that is the most contemptible approach.

## Change How You Think

- Defining a problem is an advanced skill. Build a full loop early: discover the problem, define it, solve it, and prevent it from recurring.
- Let value guide what you decide to do, results guide how you do it, and problems guide how you explain it.
- If you can't explain something clearly, it is probably not because you're a hands-on person; you may not have thought it through. This becomes especially obvious in promotion reviews.
- When someone is good at solving problems in a particular context, they may become increasingly tied to that context over time. It's hard to shed a label once people have applied it.

## Keep Your Emotions in Check

- Learn to manage your emotions. People rarely listen carefully to someone who is angry.
- Even when hurt or furious, stay rational. Don't become someone who always needs to be soothed.
- Truly confident people can admit their own mistakes. Sometimes we get angry because someone has exposed an insecurity we have tried to hide.
- What hurts us most is neither what others do nor the mistakes we make, but how we respond to those mistakes.

## Become a Leader

> A manager has direct reports; a leader has followers. Not everyone needs to be a manager, but anyone can be a leader.

- The person you trust and choose to follow isn't necessarily your manager; it may be the person who helps you. The same is true if you want others to trust and follow you.
- Don't judge people negatively too quickly. A judgment based on limited information may be wrong, and spreading it casually can cause serious trouble for someone else.
- A leader who doesn't believe in the company's mission, vision, and values will have a difficult time.
- Don't lose faith in your teammates when things get hard. Give timely, positive feedback.
- A captain's most important job isn't building a ship; it is inspiring the crew to long for the sea.
- A leader's basic responsibility is keeping the team going. That requires achieving the goals set by managers, owners, and company executives—a principle that becomes clearest in difficult times.
- A leader must identify what the team needs to do and hold steady so everyone can move forward together. This matters even more when times are hard.
- Make everyone you meet feel important and needed.
