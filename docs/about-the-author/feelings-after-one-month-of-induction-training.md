I've been at the company for just over a month. Before joining, I had never interned or worked at a company, so many things about starting my first job were new to me. Moving from school to the workplace means taking on a different role, and the difference varies from person to person. At school, I could choose which lessons to focus on based on my interests, and even skip classes I didn't want to attend. Work is different: if the company needs me to learn a skill, I have to learn it unless I want to leave. At school, most people program to pass exams or find a good job, and relatively few do it purely out of interest. At work, we program because the job requires it, and the work is usually more challenging and stressful. At school, we are mainly responsible for ourselves and build up our knowledge. At work, we are responsible for the company too; it pays us to contribute, not to spend all day “on the beach.”

When I joined, the company asked me to switch to a Mac. I had always used Windows, so it felt very unfamiliar. At first I was clumsy with macOS, and I could feel my programming productivity drop by at least 30 percent. I was frustrated and kept wondering why I couldn't use Windows or Linux instead. Strangely, after about a week I started to get used to coding on a Mac and even came to like it. I won't compare Mac and Windows as development environments; it depends on the person. At the same price, Macs do tend to have much better hardware than Windows machines. The development and everyday experience on a Mac is good, though Linux is also a fine choice. One more thing: Macs can't run some popular online games, which may help people who have trouble resisting the urge to play.

ThoughtWorks has a good training program. New graduates usually receive training after joining. Unlike previous years, this year's program included a local China cohort, TWU-C. As one of its first participants, I found it very worthwhile. The eight-week program covered core technologies used at work, such as ES6 and Spring Boot, as well as skills for new employees: running effective meetings, giving useful feedback, refactoring code, and practicing test-driven development (TDD). There were occasional activities, including weekend trips, city tours, and cake time. The final three weeks were devoted to a realistic project simulation, much like an actual work project. We have since completed one iteration of the project. What I learned most was not the technology itself, but how to work as a team, collaborate with Git, understand a complete iteration, deal with project challenges, and follow an entire project workflow.

ThoughtWorks strongly encourages sharing and helping others grow, and I've seen that firsthand. During training, each of us had a trainer—a colleague who taught classes and guided projects—usually responsible for five or six people. Trainers gave us feedback on our recent work from time to time. It wasn't just a formality; they took it seriously and often spoke with us after work. My colleagues are helpful too. If you ask a question and someone knows the answer, they'll usually share it openly. If no one knows, they may organize a technical session to explore it together. Last Friday, I gave my team a session on Feign remote calls. The team wasn't familiar with the topic, and our upcoming project would probably need it. I had just looked into it, so I shared what I learned to help the project move forward.

ThoughtWorks also values a culture of feedback. Feedback means telling people how you see their work and what they can do to improve. At first I didn't pay much attention to it, but over time I realized how helpful good feedback can be. When we do something, we may miss small issues that are obvious to others. For example, imagine there is no dedicated tester on a project. You finish your module and test it over and over until it seems fine. Yet when someone actually uses it, they may find a problem you never noticed. Everyone has blind spots shaped by what they focus on. We can miss issues in our own work even after testing it repeatedly, while another person may spot them right away.

Since starting work, I've had less time to update my public account and column or maintain GitHub. In reality, I often have time for personal projects after work, but I make excuses: I'm tired, or my free time comes in short blocks. Today I checked GitHub and realized that I had left someone else's pull request unanswered for 14 days. I should have managed my time better. There are many things I want to write about, and I'll make time for them gradually. Working has made me realize how important those few hours after work can be. If you didn't finish your work during the day, you can continue then. If your workload is manageable, you can spend that time on things you enjoy or technologies you want to learn. Whatever you choose, be realistic about your current level and don't aim too far beyond it.

There are many talented people at work, and I think everyone should learn from those around them. Some of my training cohort are strong technically; others stand out for their organization and teamwork. One colleague was especially impressive. While the rest of us were learning Spring Boot syntax, he spent his free time writing a simplified version of Spring Boot, including common Spring annotations such as `@RestController`, `@Autowired`, `@PathVariable`, and `@RequestParam`. (I've asked him to open-source it and will share it on the public account as soon as he does—stay tuned!) He seems truly passionate about programming. He started in middle school and is deeply interested in how things work, having implemented many low-level components himself. His goal is to build an open-source project with more than 20K GitHub stars. I believe he can do it, and I hope he reaches that goal soon.

These are my personal reflections after just over a month at the company. I have only touched on many things; later, I'll make time to share useful things I learn at work and in my spare time. I hope readers find them helpful.
