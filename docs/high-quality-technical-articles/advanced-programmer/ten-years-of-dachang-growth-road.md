Recently, several younger colleagues have talked with me about feeling uncertain: some about technical growth, some about promotion, and others about choosing a career path. I am glad they are willing to hear advice from someone who has been through it.

I joined a large tech company after graduating and worked in the industry for 12 years. Everything I share comes from my own experience or what I have seen around me. In particular, I will combine **the mistakes I made** with **the qualities I have observed in excellent engineers** to offer advice.

I believe these lessons apply broadly. Whether you work at a large or small company, following them may help you sooner or later.

I took some wrong turns during my 12 years of work, so I want to discuss what to keep in mind over a technical career's first decade. There are two parts:

1. **How to grow as an engineer over ten years**
2. **Some important career choices**

## 01. How to Grow as an Engineer Over Ten Years

### Years 1–2: From Beginner to Professional

New graduates often face an adjustment period. Their code may be sent back for revisions again and again. Technical discussions with experienced teammates may leave them confused. They may be afraid to ask questions or challenge ideas, or spend too long struggling with a problem alone.

**In short, even if you work day and night, you may still struggle to get the job done.**

The most important things at this stage are:

**Observe and learn by example:** When writing code, do not focus only on whether it works, as you might with a school assignment. Pay attention to module design, error handling, readability, and other concerns. Before you fully understand the reasoning behind these practices, learn by following good examples. Over time, you will understand how production code is written and why.

The same goes for technical proposals. You may not yet understand every requirement for a design document, but you can study existing documents and use them as a guide.

**Do not be afraid to ask:** People understand that you are new. Ask when you do not know something. You can also ask experienced teammates to review your proposals; do not worry about looking foolish.

**Pay attention to how work gets done:** Raise risks early when a task is unlikely to meet its deadline. Report progress regularly, for example in a weekly update. Record decisions and action items after meetings. Do your best to meet commitments, and follow company requirements such as release and access-control procedures.

After about two years, you should be a reliable professional. Your manager should be able to trust you with a task without unexpected surprises—for example, learning at the last minute that an important feature cannot be released tomorrow as planned.

### Years 3–4: From Professional to Go-To Engineer

After two years, you should have a solid understanding of the business and its systems. Engineers then begin taking on harder technical challenges.

For example, you might need to improve system performance to a specific target, refactor an important module, or coordinate a major project across several teams.

These problems are much harder than ordinary feature requests. Solving them requires technical skills as well as stronger collaboration skills.

The most important things at this stage are:

**Improve your technical skills:** Actively study technical topics, both inside and outside the company. Challenges at this stage often involve **performance**, **reliability**, and **extensibility**—areas with established practices and methods across the industry.

**Take ownership:** Technical challenges require more than designing and implementing a solution. They also involve monitoring results after launch, understanding upstream and downstream changes and risks for major projects, and planning follow-up improvements in later phases.

After about four years, you may become one of the team's primary technical contributors. Even when you are not implementing a solution yourself, you may decide on the approach. You will research options, compare designs, and consider the full lifecycle of a technical improvement.

### Years 5–7: From Go-To Engineer to Domain Expert

A go-to engineer solves a specific technical problem or handles a major project. The next step is to take responsibility for an entire business area as a domain expert.

Doing this requires **deep knowledge of the business domain and the ability to use that knowledge to plan its technical direction**.

Consider payments. A basic payment feature may be simple: call the interfaces of the payment networks, such as NetsUnion or China UnionPay, and handle success, failure, and exceptions. In many contexts, however, payments are more complicated.

Payments are sensitive to users and require a good experience. How can that experience be maintained when payment interfaces are unreliable? Payment providers charge fees, and synchronous and asynchronous interfaces may have different costs. How can those costs be reduced? Payment interfaces may also impose transaction limits. Addressing these concerns involves asynchronous processing, compensation, fund flows, and eventual consistency, among other design decisions.

The most important things at this stage are:

**Understand the industry and its trends:** Follow changes in the industry, such as new business models, policy changes, competitors' strategies, and the effects of technology and other external factors. Stay in close contact with business teams.

**Study solutions used across the industry:** Compare existing solutions in China and elsewhere. Study and try them in depth, assess development and operations costs, and make plans that account for business trends.

### Years 8–10: From Expert to Technical Leader

In many organizations, a domain expert already acts as a technical leader (TL), but that does not necessarily mean they are carrying out the role's responsibilities.

A domain expert may be able to plan technical development around business needs, but how can those plans be put into practice? One person cannot build everything alone. Here, TL refers less to a management title and more to **using a team's combined effort to carry out a technical plan**.

A TL needs skills such as developing the team's technical capabilities, allocating resources well, setting priorities, and motivating and rewarding people.

The most important things at this stage are:

**Learn how to manage:** This does not mean manipulating people. It means understanding each team member's goals and finding ways to align them with the team's goals so everyone can move forward.

**Stay grounded in technology:** It is easy to lose touch with technology when work shifts toward management. But a strong technical leader remains a strong engineer. Taking part in technical discussions, offering guidance, broadening your technical knowledge, and staying curious are key to maintaining the team's respect and engagement.

## 02. Some Important Career Choices

Here are some difficult choices you may face over the course of a decade. These lessons come from real successes and failures.

### Should I Transfer to Another Team?

Large tech companies often allow internal transfers. They can help employees explore an area they are interested in and help newer teams recruit people who can contribute right away.

An internal transfer may look like a simple change within the company, but consider it carefully.

I transferred several times. Even within the same company, a transfer is similar to changing jobs. You may have to start over in building domain knowledge, learning the work, establishing trust, and building working relationships.

My advice: **If you want to broaden your technical experience and skills, a transfer may be a good choice. If your main goal is promotion, I would not recommend it.** Promotion usually depends on sustained contributions in one area and trust built within a team.

There may be other reasons to transfer, such as family or health circumstances; I will not discuss those here.

### Should I Change Jobs?

Many factors can lead someone to change jobs, just as they can lead to an internal transfer. There is no single answer, so here are a few situations:

**You were not promoted:** Be honest with yourself. If you still need to grow into the role, keep working toward it. If you think the panel's decision was unfair, try interviewing elsewhere and see how the job market evaluates you.

**Your growth has stalled:** You may feel that your work is no longer challenging. Talk with your manager. You may not see the challenges in your current work, or your manager may not know that you want more responsibility.

**The work environment does not suit you:** This often happens when you join a new company or get a new manager. Some discomfort is normal. My advice: **If the environment focuses on the work rather than the person, consider staying and adapting.** The friction may come from different working styles. But if the environment makes things personal, it may be time to leave.

### What Kind of Job Should I Look for?

When changing jobs, we often interview with several companies at once. In a strong job market, you may receive several offers. How should you choose?

Common factors include **company reputation**, **compensation**, **job level and title**, and **technical environment**. Everyone has different priorities, so there is no universally right choice.

My advice is to **focus on whether the new role offers room to grow in a way that meets your expectations**.

For example, if you want to become an architect, will the role offer enough technical challenges to help you grow, or will you spend all your time responding to requests?

If you want to move into technical management, will you have a chance to lead people? Are there enough problems to solve that require building a team?

If you want to specialize in a domain such as e-commerce or gaming, will the role let you continue in that field and work on a wider range of its problems?

Of course, if the compensation is too good to turn down, you can set these considerations aside!

## Closing Thoughts

These are my thoughts on a decade of growth for technical professionals in the tech industry. I hope they help when you feel uncertain or face an important choice. If even a few of my words help you at some point in the future, I will be honored.
